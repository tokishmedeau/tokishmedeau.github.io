CHIVEY

Chapter I
Tokish J. Médeau

One day the Sun said to the Moon,

“My sister! Let us trade places.”

The Moon answered with a sigh,

“Can you bear the tears people shed at night?”

The Sun looked at the Moon and replied,

“Can you bear the weight of those

who wait for night to ease their grief?”

Tokish J. Médeau

Who invented these faux leather chairs? When it's hot, you literally stick to the plastic, your skin slides around on your own sweat, and you slip right down off the seat. And when it's cold... when it's cold, you're just cold. Like right now. The stupid air conditioner hangs right across from me, blasting cold air straight at me. Why would anyone turn it up that high...?

The cubicle shuddered and slowly began to crawl apart in different directions, doubling objects and blurring their outlines, multiplying the folds of the curtains into endless stripes. They closed in on Emmy from every side, driving her into the corner of the awkward chair, against its uncomfortable armrest upholstered in mint-colored vinyl. The whitish streaks on the surface, left behind by relentless sterility, rolled into little pills beneath her fingertips and spread out across the surface again, unable to tear themselves away from it.

“Tss... ouch!” She winced and pulled her hand back sharply, then raised her wrist to her face to examine the puncture. A small, dark spot formed around it, its outline resembling a tiny mouse.

Great… another bruise.

The space snapped back into place, returning everything to its proper position. A drop fell from the tip of the needle onto the white cotton of her stretched-out wife-beater, the dark red bead sitting on the fabric for a moment, catching the deathly pale light of the lamp. Emmy carefully touched the drop, trying to brush it away, but only smeared it, leaving a conspicuous stain.

“Look what you did! You ruined my wife-beater…”

The surgical mask swelled with a deep breath, a pair of dark eyes, heavily lined in black pencil, narrowed and fixed on her.

“Really? You’re serious right now? What is there to ruin? That T-shirt belongs in the trash.” Carmen smoothed her sleek hair back with the back of her hand and wiped away a bead of sweat that had formed on her forehead. Closing her fingers around Emmy’s wrist, she pulled her closer. “Chivey! Sit still, don’t squirm! You’re always such a handful … Just one IV, that’s all.”

Wrenching her hand from the nurse’s grip, Emmy shifted into the other corner of the chair, drawing her feet up onto it. Pressing her knees to her chin, she thrust one leg out threateningly, shod in a heavy leather boot.

“Leave me alone! I’ll kick you! I said no. And what am I supposed to do about this stain? It’ll never come out now…”

The wheels of the stool squeaked softly as Carmen abruptly pushed herself backward. Without taking her eyes off Emmy, she yanked the mask from her face and tossed it into the wastebasket. She peeled off her latex gloves, turning them inside out, and tossed them after the mask. Biting her lower lip, she pulled a marker from her breast pocket and flicked the cap off with her thumb.

“I’m so sick of you…” she whispered under her breath. “Move closer. I’ll fix your shirt problem.” She smoothed the hem of Emmy’s T-shirt over her knee and bent her head, studying the stain intently. Then, with quick, confident strokes, she turned the red blotch into a strawberry. Catching Emmy’s angry glare, she gave her a sly smile. “There. Problem solved? What shall we do next? Give it another try?”

“No, it’s not solved. Go get Chad. At least he knows how to find a vein and start an IV. Unlike you.” Emmy glanced at the strawberry and tugged the hem of her T-shirt down over her knees. “I’m sick of you too…”

The wheels of the stool whispered across the tiled floor as Carmen rolled away. Carmen stood up, straightened her scrubs, adjusted her hair, and looked down at Emmy. “Fine. You win. You’ve beaten me this time. Sit still. I’ll go find Chad.” She picked the cap up off the floor, snapped it back onto the marker, and slipped the marker into her pocket.

“Sick of you…” Leaving Emmy to her proud solitude, Carmen walked away, pointedly drawing the curtain shut behind her.

“Respect.” Emmy flinched at the unexpected voice; a low, raspy voice from the neighboring cubicle trailed off into a fit of coughing. “Respect is what people are lacking these days. Where is the world heading? Oh, Lord, forgive this foolish child…” The voice faded to a whisper, nestling among the folds of the curtain. Emmy scowled, rubbing the fresh bruise on her arm with one finger.

Why am I the one getting blamed? She’s the one who’s angry… She ruined my T-shirt, drew some stupid little berry on it. What am I, a little kid, that she has to draw pictures on my clothes? And now she probably thinks I’m impossible to deal with altogether. It’s not fair. My favorite wife-beater, my cozy white T-shirt, all stretched out already…

I wonder why they even call them wife-beaters? What, is this shirt supposed to have beaten up its own wife? That’s just stupid…

Leaning back in the chair, she raised her arms high above her head and stretched, arching her back and cracking the joints in her fingers. Then she brought her hands closer to her face and interlaced her fingers. The cold light of the fluorescent lamp filtered through her interlaced fingers, following their slow movements and casting jagged glints across her face; the patterns of light shifted, forming strange ornaments. Enchanted by the play of light, she searched for fantastical shadow figures through the shifting kaleidoscope of gaps between her fingers, swaying in the breath of the air conditioner.

The game didn't last long. Losing interest, Emmy yawned widely; her feet began to move restlessly, tapping out a familiar tune with the toes of her boots.

Shine, shine, shine. Let it shine…
Shine, shine, shine. Let it shine…
So let it shine…

Humming quietly under her breath, she listened to the tapping of her boots, keeping time with soft claps of her hands.

A soft whistle slipped through the folds of the curtain, rising and fading among the wavering shadows of the fabric, accompanying her faint voice as it slid and wove itself into the melody. The edge of the curtain suddenly swept aside, and a head appeared in the opening, grinning from ear to ear, all thirty-two teeth on display. Chad stepped into the cubicle and stopped in front of Emmy, pointedly planting his hands on his hips.

“And who do we have here?” He pulled a candy from his pocket, deftly flipped it from hand to hand, then closed his fist around it. “Guess which hand?”

“Come on. I’m not six.” She wrinkled her nose and rolled her eyes.

“Are you sure about that? A little angry bird told me someone in here is acting up. Like a small child.” He sat down on the chair and tossed the candy onto Emmy’s lap.

“Oh, indeed, just like a little child. This little lady has certainly been giving everyone a hard time. Forgive her, Lord…” The voice behind the curtain broke off in a cough, then came to life again. “Chad, sweetheart, is that you?”

Reaching for the curtain, Chad leaned over to peer into the neighboring cubicle. “Good afternoon, Mrs. Jackson. How are you feeling?”

“I'm fine, child, just fine. Still alive and kicking, the Lord has been good to me. Come by later, won't you? Give an old lady a little attention…”

“Of course, Mrs. Jackson. Of course…” He squeezed some sanitizer from the dispenser on the table and thoroughly rubbed the liquid into his palms, working it into every crease of his skin; then he glanced at Emmy and shook his hands to let them dry. “Well then, little rebel, shall we get down to business?” Latex gloves, taken from a fresh packet, fitted snugly over his hands, tracing the contours of the prominent veins beneath his skin; the sterile needle glinted in the light of the lamp.

“Give me your arm, and we'll gently coax those shy little veins out of hiding. Look how timid they are, hiding behind all those bruises…” He wrapped his hand around her forearm and eased the needle in; drop by drop the cold solution began moving through her vein, and a strip of medical tape held it in place. Emmy's fingers lightly brushed his skin.

My arm is like a pale fish on black sand.

“And that's it. See? What a wonderful team we've made. Now sit quietly, like a little mouse. I'll be right back.”

Her fingers slid along his hand, and Emmy caught Chad by the palm.

"You are black, but comely."

“Hold on…” Chad’s face flushed; his mouth dropped open into a perfectly round “O.” “Hold on. Seriously? Black, but comely?” He slowly peeled off the gloves and, closing his eyes, rubbed the bridge of his nose. His shoulders shook and a bubbling laugh burst out.

“Baby, you should do stand-up! Black, but comely… Hnk-ggh… Black! But comely!” The gloves flew into the wastebasket; he reached out and snatched the candy from the hem of Emmy’s lap and tucked it into his pocket. On his way out he yanked the curtain aside and, turning back to Emmy, wagged a finger at her.

“Sit still! And don’t even think about running off. Black, but comely…” He went down the corridor, stopping now and then to snap his fingers. “Black… but comely.”

Emmy frowned and bit her lower lip.

So much for compliments after this… No thank you, no nothing.

From behind the curtain came a racking cough; the fabric slid aside and a pair of large lenses flashed in the depths of the neighboring cubicle. Pushing her glasses down to the tip of her nose, the elderly woman looked Emmy over, studying her face intently.

“Hah, sweetie! Black, but comely?! You did not just say that… No, no, I cannot believe what I just heard. Nobody would believe me even if I swore on a Bible. Lord... a little white child quoting the Song of Solomon... How old are you — nine? Ten?” She adjusted the silk scarf wound elaborately around her head. “I am black, but comely, O ye daughters of Jerusalem, as the tents of Kedar, as the curtains of Solomon. Isn’t that a little advanced for you?”

“I’m not ten at all. I’m almost fourteen. I read what I find.” Pulling the hem of her shirt down over her knees, Emmy pressed herself deeper into the chair, trying to merge with it.

“Fourteen? You don’t look fourteen. Like a little fledgling.” Opening a small purse, Mrs. Jackson pulled out a white handkerchief, unhurriedly took off her glasses and began polishing the lenses, now and then fogging them with her breath. “Oh, at your age I read everything too. Everything — newspapers, magazines, the Bible of course, goes without saying. I even read my older sister’s medical textbooks. I was a very well-read child. Very well-read!”

The handkerchief fluttered like a butterfly, never resting for a moment, and neither did the woman; she talked and talked, no pauses, no waiting for an answer. The hand in front of her face, with the glasses clutched in it, swung in unison, underscoring the importance of her words.

If she stops, she'll die.

Mrs. Jackson’s words fell from her mouth, weaving themselves into the lace of her batiste dress. Fading and flaring, they trickled down a necklace of large, uneven turquoise beads, slipped into the folds of fabric around her shrunken chest and, sliding over legs with swollen, knotted veins, dropped to the floor and shattered against the cold tile. From there her voice spread out through both cubicles and enveloped everything around, like an unbroken stream.

“Everybody’s long gone, every last one... My daughter Annette moved to the city with the grandkids... And you know what? I don’t miss them, I definitely don’t miss them. Understand? I’ve got plenty to keep me busy, I definitely have plenty to keep me busy. Now what do you like? Hm? Me, I like potato salad. I use only yellow potatoes, only yellow. And eggs — real eggs, farm eggs. I drive out to my cousin Janet; she has the most wonderful hens. Oh, what hens! What large eggs!”

Oh, come on... Potato salad? How boring. Raisins... Don’t forget the raisins.

Covering her mouth with her hand, Emmy yawned wide. Her gaze slid along the turquoise beads and drifted upward, past the wrinkled neck and the stretched earlobe to the silk scarf: against its soft blue ground bright flowers wove into a motley pattern, gathering into a lush wreath. Having admired it to her heart’s content, Emmy began counting the smallest, unopened buds, but, losing count, she stopped.

If I take four long sticks and tape them to my head with duct tape, and then take a scarf like this one, and another scarf, and another one, and another... and wind them around the sticks, wind and wind... I'd be two Emmys tall. I'd be taller than everyone... and I'd probably reach the ceiling with my scarves. Maybe I'd even look like a supermodel. Fashionable...

She closed her eyes and shifted her heavy boots, pacing out an invisible runway. A proud smile slipped across her lips.

“You know, I have a whole room set aside for my wardrobe. What isn’t in there: dresses, shoes, purses... And the hats! Do you know how many hats I have? Dozens, I keep them in boxes. Though these days my head is mostly adorned with scarves, well, you understand... Truly, every outfit suits me, because I am black, but comely. Every Sunday service is a celebration, a little performance, and it takes a sense of style, a special one. A dress of crisp taffeta with a wide ruffle along the hem. And a little hat... Real chic! Lord, how I love all these occasions... And do you go to Sunday service?” No answer came. The woman adjusted her scarf and stared at Emmy. Realizing she was being ignored, she fell silent, offended.

The chair swayed. The lamps in the cubicles blinked and went out, then glowed again. Something lifted Emmy and carried her away: sliding across the damp vinyl, she flew like a weightless puff of down into the blue scarf. The flowers parted. Green valleys, high hills overgrown with spreading trees, crystal-clear lakes rushed by beneath her feet. She came down smoothly, and the tips of her bare toes touched the tender petals of wildflowers. The heavy fragrance of a blooming meadow struck her nose.

It was that simple. All I needed was a little push with my foot, and now I can fly...

Fly... Fly...

Fly...

“Here I am.” The flowers flinched and winked out, shedding their petals as the dream dissolved, leaving Emmy slumped deep in the chair. "Time to set you free, little sparrow." Chad drew the needle out and pressed a cotton pad to her skin.

"Now you're truly free. Can you walk on your own?" She slid slowly off the chair and, bending her arm at the elbow, made her way toward the exit of the cubicle.

"There... Over there, on the floor behind the chair, you've forgotten something. A sweater or what is that?" Mrs. Jackson pointed a finger at a black cardigan carelessly tossed on the floor.

"Yours?" Chad picked up the cardigan and held it out to Emmy, who nodded. "You'll lose everything at this rate, Emmy-Dizzy-Penny!"

"Emmy-Dizzy-Penny..." Mrs. Jackson shivered, pulling the collar of her dress up to her neck. "Not dizzy at all, Penny had the sense to bring something warm. That air conditioner is going to be the death of me. Chad, sweetheart, can it be turned off? Or at least bring me a warm blanket. I'm not complaining, Jesus gives me strength, but it's so cold... It's so cold in here!" She adjusted the scarf on her head and fixed the nurse with a steady look, her brows pitched into a little roof, the corners of her mouth drooping mournfully.

"I'll bring you a blanket, Mrs. Jackson. I'll just walk the young lady out to the corridor and come right back." Chad draped the cardigan over Emmy's shoulders and fastened the top button. "Just a moment.”

Mrs. Jackson settled back in her chair. "God bless your kind heart, child." She raised her hand, palm open.

A row of fluorescent lights drifted over Emmy's head, fixture after fixture, merging in the distance into a single line that ended in a door with glass panels, through which warm sunlight broke. One after another the lamps crawled across the perforated ceiling panels, trembling nervously. One of them flared brightly and went out, and Chad glanced up at it. "Probably burned out. Or the wiring's acting up. This wing of the building is really old, anything can happen."

Emmy stopped, swept her gaze across the ceiling and pressed against Chad, wrapping her arms around his leg. "Those holes… Little dark holes… it's like they're watching us. Maybe hungry rats chewed through the wire up there?"

He laughed, freeing himself from Emmy's clinging arms. "What an imagination! There's nothing up there but wires and pipes. Maybe a couple of centipedes hiding… What, are you afraid of insects, silly?"

Insects?.. Oh no… What if somebody lives up there, behind the ceiling, in those holes? All those tiny clinging legs crawling across my head… Yuck! Disgusting. Absolutely revolting. What if there are swarms of spiders… whole nests of squirming legs… They'll fall from the ceiling and bite me…

She shuddered. "What if something nasty falls out of those holes? Right onto my head?"

Chad laid his palm on her head. "Don't be afraid. Here's my hand, it'll chase every uninvited guest off your head. Shall we?" They moved toward the sunlight. Behind them the dead lamp blinked and glowed again; Chad glanced back. "See, everything's fine. Just a short circuit. No rats and no centipedes."

The door swung open. The wide corridor was flooded with sunlight, falling in slanting columns through a row of large windows. The corridor went about its drowsy, unhurried life: patients walked past without haste, staff headed somewhere, everyone occupied with their own affairs.

"I'll sit you right here, by this window. You like looking out at the yard, don't you?" He carefully settled her onto a long low bench. "Wait here, don't go anywhere. Carmen will come for you soon and take you to Dr. Harper." Straightening the cardigan on her shoulders, he left.

A heavy buzzing sounded somewhere close: a large fly swept over her head.

You're the last thing I needed here…

Sunlight falling from the window spilled its warmth generously, bringing on a drowse. Emmy shrugged off her cardigan, folded it carelessly into thirds and tucked it under her head, stretching out on the bench. Her palms came to rest on her stomach, her fingers intertwined.

Sun. If I lie here long enough my skin will darken, get tanned, and I'll stop looking like a pale ghost. I'll be black, but comely, like the tents of Kedar, like the curtains of Solomon... Why tents and curtains? I could have compared myself to flowers... Very strange.

She closed her eyes. Tiny legs ran across her cheek, making their way toward the corner of her eye. She swept her palm across her face, brushing off the uninvited guest. A few seconds later, those tiny legs were running boldly across her bare head.

"Go... Go away! You're so annoying..."

She sat up, waving the fly off with her hand, but in vain: the fly settled comfortably on her knee, basking in the sunlight. It looked at her, shifting from one leg to another.

"No. No... No! Get lost..."

She flicked the pest away in disgust. But it wouldn't quit: it flitted from place to place, twitching its wings and tickling her skin with its clinging feet. Emmy jumped up, snatched the cardigan from the bench and began swinging it, trying to drive the fly away. Soon it disappeared from view.

Finally... Finally that pest is gone. Just as clingy as Mrs. Jackson...

An irregular buzzing sounded nearby. Emmy spotted the fly over by the book rack that stood forlornly in the wall space between two windows — on a shelf, on one of the books. She crept closer, then shot her hand forward and pinned the fly beneath her palm. She slowly lifted her fingers one by one. Empty. But under her hand, on the book cover, a small white mouse gazed up at her mournfully.

She scanned the area, searching for the little menace — she spotted it on a book display, sitting on a book. Emmy crept slowly toward the stand. She shot her hand out and cupped it over the fly, pressing her palm carefully against the book.

"Tommy..." she read aloud.

Casting a quick glance around, she took the book from the shelf and, hugging it to her chest, went back to the bench. The cardigan settled over her knees, hiding the book from prying eyes. Hooking a fingernail under the shrink wrap, she tore it off, crumpled it into a ball and quickly shoved it under the bench. Emmy sighed, slid the cardigan aside and opened the book to its first page.

The heat weighed down on the meadow like a heavy paw, driving Tommy toward the river. The little degu settled into the shade of a thick bush growing by the cool water. Paws busily groomed white fur. Prickly burdock seeds stung his skin, sending an unbearable itch down his back.

Where did all of you come from? Oh, here’s another one stuck in the tuft of the tail…

He yawned and swallowed. The sweet smell of strawberries drifted through the air, teasing him, moistening his mouth.

The little one remembered his father’s rule against sweets perfectly well. But the strawberry bushes were so close he could see ruby berries gleaming above the dark leaves. He imagined the sweet-tart burst on his tongue — and that was enough to tip the scales.

Keeping low, he darted across the worn path and vanished into the green shadow of the bushes. He devoured everything within reach — berries, stems, leaves, even small distracted insects. Sticky juice splashed and ran down his dusty snout and paws.

A loud burp escaped him. He froze."

Emmy snapped the book shut.

Oh, this is for little kids. A mouse, a strawberry… Strawberries… I could go for some strawberries right now. The little sweet ones. Though… a degu. What's a degu, anyway? Just a mouse with a tassel on its tail. They could've just called it a mouse. When was the last time I even read a little kids' book…?

She set the book down on the bench beside her.

Her leg had gone numb. An unpleasant tingling spread through it. Emmy rubbed her leg with her palms, then stood and walked back and forth beside the bench.

Suddenly, on one of the ceramic floor tiles, she slipped, caught herself at the last moment and stayed on her feet. She looked down.

Where did this water come from? A water pipe must’ve burst somewhere... Or maybe not... If a pipe had burst, water would be all over the place.

She stepped on the tile. It gave smoothly downward and sprang back up. Clear water splashed out from underneath. Emmy reached into her cardigan pocket and pulled out a nail file. She crouched down, wedged the file under the edge of the tile and lifted it, moving it aside. The smell of rotted wood hit her nostrils. Sunlight slanted in through the gap, driving the shadows back.

The blackened wooden floor was covered in a thin layer of water. In a deep hollow lay a shiny white shell dotted with red.

Emmy dipped her fingers in the water and pulled the shell out. Its sharp edges pressed into the pads of her fingers, its ridges masking the dampness. She got to her feet and walked to the window, set the shell on the sill.

How did it get here? Who put that poor little shell on the floor...? Whose dumb idea was that?

A sharp ringing in her ear made her double over in pain. She squeezed her eyes shut, mouth opening in a silent cry.

The ringing stopped. Emmy opened her eyes. The shell was gone. In its place on the windowsill lay a cotton pad speckled with dried blood. She looked back at the floor. There was no trace of the wooden boards or the water. She prodded the tile with the toe of her boot, but not one of them moved, fixed solid to the floor. Emmy rubbed her eyes with her fingers.

That was strange... Very strange...

She went back to the bench. Looked at the floor once more, tapped her heel against the nearest tiles.

Nothing there. Fine.

She went back to her reading.

"He squeezed his eyes shut. A heavy wave rolled through his stomach, and the sweet scent of strawberries suddenly turned sour.

“Tommy! Tooommy! Where are you, you naughty boy?” Father’s voice rang out in the distance.

Caught off guard, Tommy bolted. He dropped low into the grass and darted toward the river. He had no desire to dip his paws into the cool water, but berry juice gleamed in bright stains against his white fur. The water didn’t help — the juice had already soaked in, leaving pale pink streaks behind.

Father’s voice drew closer.

Tommy’s heart began to race. He slipped into the thick sedge and froze. A plan formed quickly. He bit off a few young stems, gathered them in an armful, and pressed them to his chest.

“Tommy, where are you?” Father’s voice sounded tired.

“Dad, I’m here!” Tommy called, stepping out of the sedge. He moved slowly toward Father, hiding his pink-stained muzzle behind the bundle of grass..."

Something snapped inside her.

Stupid story.

She threw the book behind the bench. Leaned forward on her knees and buried her face in her hands.

"I'm feeding you and that brat, don't you forget it! Remember who's in charge here!"

The smell of alcohol hit her in the face. Emmy pushed the memory away. Leaning back, she reached down behind the bench, felt around for the book and pulled it out.

It's not your fault… Really, not your fault…

She set the book down beside her.

Relax... Just relax. Everything's fine... It's completely fine.

Emmy waved her palm in front of her face. The legs moved up onto her bare head.

How many times do I have to — you are so annoying.

She sat up sharply, grabbed the cardigan and threw it over her head. Left a small gap in front of her face. Swatting at the fly, Emmy settled in to watch the reception desk.

Same as always. Same faces. I know all of them by now. Every time they do the same thing. That one's about to beg for a pen and paper, and that one smiles at everyone and makes small talk.

And there's Carmen. With that same stiff smile and perfect hair. Can't stand her.

Emmy glanced down at the hem of her shirt.

She does have an imagination though. Drew a strawberry...

Emmy’s gaze drifted down the corridor and landed on Chad pushing Mrs. Jackson in her wheelchair. She lifted a hand in greeting, and he smiled back, easy and brief.

At the far end of the hall she noticed a familiar silhouette in a white coat. Sunlight from the windows caught his dark hair and turned it copper at the edges.

That's him. Double-H.

She pulled the cardigan off her head and smoothed herself down—straps, hem, a quick nervous adjustment. Then she paused, cheeks warming without permission.

Dr. Harper was walking toward them, greeting patients and staff as he passed. His smile was clean and bright; when he reached Chad and Mrs. Jackson, he folded himself down almost in half to speak with her.

Emmy lifted her hand again. He didn’t see her.

Something in the corridor shifted. A faint ringing started behind her ears and swelled until it seemed to fill the space between the walls. The ceiling light flickered, flared, and then snapped out with a sharp crack. A scatter of tiny sparks drifted down.

Nothing around her reacted. The corridor kept moving as if it had never happened.

She found him again at the reception desk, speaking with the nurses. One of them held out a folder, talking quickly and pointing to something inside it, then gestured toward Emmy. For a moment his head turned, and his eyes passed over her direction. Emmy waved—smaller this time—and still nothing.

She winced, pressed her lips together, then stood up too quickly and broke into a run.

"Dr. Harper! Dr. Harper!"

The fluorescent lights along the corridor switched on and began flickering wildly, bursting one after another.

The sharp ringing in her ears came back. It swelled and then faded to almost nothing, then swelled again. Emmy stopped and pressed her palms over her ears. The ringing didn't go away. It grew stronger, filling everything around her.

A hard shove knocked Emmy off her feet. She hit the floor, the tile slick with water that had come from nowhere.

The hospital building let out a long groan as a faint tremor ran through the floor and walls. Emmy pressed her palms to the ground and sat up, looking around as jagged cracks spread across the walls and plaster broke away in heavy chunks. The floor tiles buckled and shifted, exposing worn boards beneath them, and the dark-stained wood smelled of fish and iodine.

She got to her feet and pressed herself against the wall as the floor continued to vibrate. A second jolt hit harder. Ceiling panels came loose, light fixtures tore free and swung on tangles of wire, spinning slowly as sunlight cut through the rising dust. Then, for a breath, everything went still.

People in the corridor scrambled to help each other up, brushing debris from their clothes, anxious voices rising and overlapping.

“Everyone out, get out!”

A screech of metal cut through the noise. The building shuddered again. The floor heaved, then dropped away as the corridor tilted, and furniture and debris surged down the hall in a collapsing wave, sweeping everything before it. Screams filled the space.

“Level — GIVE FIRE!” Dr. Harper’s voice came through as if from behind thick cotton.

A tremendous crash tore through the corridor, and dense smoke poured in, filling every corner until sulfur worked its way deep into her lungs and forced a cough from her chest. Emmy pushed off the wall and moved forward through the haze, arms stretched out ahead of her, grasping at nothing as the world dissolved into gray.

Her fingers struck a body in the smoke. A short boy in a worn linen shirt stood directly in her path, clutching a leather bucket with its lid pressed hard to his chest. He stared at her for a moment with wide, unmoving eyes, then bolted, vanishing into the smoke as if it had swallowed him whole.

A strong gust of salt wind tore through the corridor and briefly cleared the air. A bitter taste of salt touched her lips and vanished just as quickly, leaving behind tiny flakes of dry, cracked skin.

A dozen feet ahead, legs planted wide, stood Dr. Harper. A broad cloak thrown over his old-fashioned coat billowed in the wind as he looked directly at Emmy and held out his hand.

“Emmanuèl!”

Emmy backed away.

Nearly knocking her down, Mrs. Jackson’s wheelchair flew past, the old woman screaming all the way. The chair slammed full force into the reception desk, and Mrs. Jackson was hurled over it, her voice cutting off mid-air as she went silent. Her body rolled slowly to a stop at Dr. Harper’s feet.

Emmy screamed. Hiding her face in her hands, she ran down the corridor away from him. Her foot caught on the leg of an overturned chair and she fell, striking her head hard against the floor. A wave swept through the corridor and carried her limp body under the reception desk, leaving her beside an overturned waste bin.

Emmy groaned and opened her eyes.

Something cold and slick brushed her arm. She looked down — a large fish lay thrashing at her feet, its long winglike fins spread across the floor, mouth opening and closing on nothing. She slowly sat up, her back against the filing cabinet, and wrapped her arms around her bruised head.

A familiar voice rang out nearby, followed by a harsh scrape of metal. The heavy cabinets along the walls shifted, then began to slide as one mass. One of them caught and pinned Carmen against the wall.

She pushed back with both hands, frantic, but the cabinet only pressed harder.

“Someone, help me! Lord, please, help me!” Her voice broke as the pressure tightened around her.

The cabinet tore free and surged forward, slamming into the counter and ripping it loose from the floor. A splinter of wood whipped across Emmy’s cheek.

“GIVE FIRE!”

Smoke closed in again. Something vast burst out of the billowing clouds toward Emmy, and she shut her eyes, drawing a deep breath.

A heavy wall of cold water crashed over her head, forcing the air from her lungs as her body fought the invisible current, salt burning in her throat. The current ground her body and spat her out onto a hard surface. Rough planks stretched beneath her like a ship’s deck, slick and worn, the smell of brine thick in the air.

“Enough of thy lounging, knave! Arise, thou milk-sop!”

Emmy opened her eyes. Bright sunlight pushed through her thick lashes, forcing her to squint. Golden specks darted around her, gathering into the shapes of people and dissolving again just as fast.

Long strands of sun-bleached, dirty-blond hair fell across her face, getting into her eyes. Emmy caught a strand between her fingers and pulled it away, water trickling down onto her wrist.

Is this mine? Hair? Do I have hair?

She dragged her whole hand through her hair.

Somebody's stupid prank… But… it's not a wig… This is my own hair! When was the last time… So long ago...

Her soaked clothes clung to her body, restricting her movements. The coarse fabric rubbed against her skin, scraping with every motion. Emmy slid her hand under the hempen shirt, pulling the wet cloth away from herself.

She looked down and went still.

A trail of soft hair extended from the waistband of her trousers, past her navel, spreading into a light dusting across her chest. Her palm moved over her stomach, across the hard ridges of muscle, then slid lower, toward her groin.

Emmy jerked her hand back, snagged on something foreign.

What is that?

Her body had changed.

This can't be happening… It can't be... Am I… dead?

Her face flushed, and a nervous giggle escaped her lips.

Emmy yanked up her sleeve. No puncture marks, no bruises. Bulging veins pulsed beneath her sun-kissed skin, right above the twitching tendons.

No, it's just a dream. Just a twisted dream…

The toe of a boot prodded her back.

"Up, boy. No time for thy slumber."

The skirts of his grimy doublet fell open. A heavy, nail-studded sole of a salt-crusted boot passed inches from her face. A wide, sky-blue silk garter with a jaunty bow, holding up a thin wool stocking, made a ridiculous contrast against the filthy, sagging mass of the garment. The puffed-out legs of his slops parted, and a strip of linen shirt, stained with filth, flashed between them. A stifling waft of sour feet and musk rolled over Emmy.

The man stepped over her and walked off, his posture a heavy sea-swagger as he planted his feet wide with each stride.

Emmy jumped to her feet and hitched up her sliding trousers. Among the chaotic tangle of folds, her fingers found a small leather pouch tied to the belt-cord.

"Chivey!" A sharp call rang out somewhere high above her head.

"Chivey…! Chivey!"

"I'm here! Here!" She ran toward the voice, peering upward and glancing around. Rising onto her toes, she skipped awkwardly between the tangled coils of rope strewn across the blackened deck planks. Sharp splinters, scattered generously everywhere, jabbed at her bare feet.

Well, where are you? How do I get out of here? Come on, let's finish this game already.

"Chivey! Cha! Cha! Kak-kak-kak…"

She stopped at the edge of the abyss, gripping the bulwark. High above her head, in a bright, wind-swept sky, gulls wheeled, screeching and cackling.

"Oh, you are…”

A wave slammed into the ship's side and threw a fan of cold spray over her. Emmy squeezed her eyes shut and wiped her face with the back of her forearm. The seawater burned like fire, the salt forcing tears from her eyes.

I'm not crying, not crying… my eyes just sting. Stupid birds…

Wiping the tears away, she glanced down. The ship surged forward, now rising above the water, now sinking into its depths. The great mass of water lived a life of its own. It swelled and breathed, built crystalline mountains, then shattered them, swallowing them whole. Dark chasms gaped their hungry mouths and were washed away in an instant by the next wave.

So this is what you look like, sea…

A low rumble knocked the top off an azure wave, turning it into a cloud of watery spray. A cannonball skimmed the water and burst it apart with a crash, spitting up a column of white foam.

"Get out of the way!"

A pair of fair-haired boys ran past her, clutching leather buckets of gunpowder tight to their chests. A crowd of sailors darted across the cramped deck, threading between the cannons, barrels, rigging, and crates. Gusts of wind carried the sharp smell of vinegar and unwashed bodies toward her.
